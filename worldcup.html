Does the World Cup move electricity prices?

Testing whether prices during match hours differ from weather-comparable days. Germany, 2026 tournament window.

No, not detectably.

Across the complete tournament, once weather and season are controlled for, prices during match hours look like prices on ordinary days, and actual demand did not measurably deviate from what the grid had forecast. One subset (overnight kickoffs) looked suggestive on the early interim data, but it faded as the window filled in, and a placebo test shows a blip that size appears by chance in random data too. The numbers below are the evidence.

How to read the numbers on this page (30 seconds)

Effect: how much higher or lower the price (or demand) was during match hours than on comparable days. The headline quantity.

t-statistic: signal-to-noise. Roughly, |t| above 2 hints at a real effect; below 2 the data is consistent with "nothing happened". Here the t's are additionally optimistic because match days share control days, which is why the placebo test matters more.

Placebo (permutation) p: the strictest check. Rerun the whole analysis on randomly chosen "match days" thousands of times; p is the share of random runs that look at least as extreme as the real one. p=0.40 means: 40% of pure-chance runs look this impressive, so the real one proves nothing. "Family-wise" corrects for having examined several subsets and reporting the best-looking one.

Minimum detectable effect (MDE): what the study could have seen. A null result only rules out effects larger than this, so every "no effect" on this page really means "no effect above the MDE".

Within-day difference-in-differences: a second estimate that compares match hours against the same day's other hours, which cancels out the season slowly shifting under the whole window. If it disagrees with the main estimate, the "effect" was drift, not the event.

The question and the method

Match viewers shift when they cook, heat, and run appliances, which changes demand. But weather (sun and wind) mostly drives price, so a fair test has to hold weather constant, and the day-ahead auction's timing decides what any price test can even detect.

One timing subtlety: the day-ahead price is set in an auction at 12:00 the day before delivery. Nothing that happens during a match can move that day's day-ahead price, so this price test measures whether traders anticipated a match effect, not whether the match caused one. The forecast-error test further down is the one that can catch a real-time surprise.

For each match day, the analysis finds the five most weather-comparable non-match days and compares the price during that day's match hours against the same clock hours on those comparable days. The average difference is the estimated match effect.

"Comparable" is defined in one place (matching.py): daily temperature mean and max, a solar proxy (radiation when available, otherwise cloud cover), and wind when available, all z-scored. Controls must also share the day type, weekday or Saturday or Sunday-or-holiday, so a public holiday never stands in for a normal weekday. See which days were compared.

The tournament runs until 19 July 2026, so results here cover matches up to the latest data fetch and will be re-run once the tournament is over.

The more direct test: did demand beat the forecast?

Prices only tell you whether the market anticipated an effect. Demand against its own forecast is the cleaner measure, because a match that moved consumption would show up as demand running above what the grid predicted, whether or not any trader saw it coming.

In real time: does the price move during match hours?

The two tests above both use prices and forecasts set before delivery, so they can only measure anticipation. This is the one test where an unanticipated shift could still show up: the reBAP, the German uniform imbalance price the four grid operators use to settle schedule deviations, forms at delivery, so a demand shift nobody priced in lands in the gap between it and the day-ahead auction.

Does any of it survive the drift control?

The comparison above runs across days, which works only if match days and comparable days are otherwise alike. They are not: the tournament window sits at a different spread level than the mostly pre-tournament controls. Once each day is compared against its own non-match hours instead, nothing survives.

Why a placebo test, and what it changes

The overnight blip survives neither the subset check nor the family-wise one, so this page reports no detectable effect. That is the method's worth: it is willing to kill a promising-looking result, and it assumes nothing about the data being bell-shaped, which matters because the t-statistics here are optimistic (match days share control days).

One subset, overnight kickoffs, produced a t-statistic just above 2, the usual line for "worth a look". Two things make that line untrustworthy here. A t of 2 is crossed by pure chance about one time in twenty, and four subsets were examined (all matches, prime time, overnight, Germany), so the chance that at least one clears the bar by luck is well above one in twenty. Reporting the best-looking of several subsets without accounting for the others is the standard way an analysis fools itself.

The placebo test measures that risk directly instead of arguing about it. It breaks the link between "match day" and "effect": random days are relabelled as fake match days, fresh weather-comparable controls are rebuilt from the remaining days, and the effect is recomputed exactly as before. Repeating this a few thousand times builds a null distribution, a picture of the effect sizes pure chance produces when nothing real is happening. The p-value is the share of those random reruns whose result is at least as extreme as the real one. A high share means chance manufactures this result routinely, so the real one proves nothing.

The multiple-subsets problem gets its own correction. Each random draw relabels the days once and carries every subset's membership through the same draw, so all four subset results come from one shared reshuffle. Taking the largest absolute t across the four, on every draw, builds a "family-wise" null, the right reference for "we looked several times, how impressive is the best result?". The real most-extreme subset is judged against that, not against its own private null.

It is the same discipline that keeps A/B tests and product experiments from shipping decisions built on noise.

This is an observational estimate, not proof of causation. Match scheduling correlates with weekends and holidays, which have their own demand patterns; the weekday-type control reduces but does not remove that. A result near zero is a real finding, not a failure.
