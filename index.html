Energy market predictability

Forecasting the German day-ahead power market, and measuring where forecasts fail. Real market data, explicit baselines, statistics that are allowed to say "no effect".

Three questions drove this project, each one raised by the answer to the last. Can you predict tomorrow's cheapest electricity hours, and what is that worth? Is the day-ahead price even the right thing to predict? And can a scheduled mega-event, the 2026 World Cup, move the market at all? The answers, computed live from the latest pipeline run:

Finding 1Can you predict it?

Smart timing is worth real money. The algorithm barely matters.

Over a full backtested year (… days), shifting a daily EV charge into the cheapest hours saves …/year versus charging anytime. A 28-day lookup table wins a six-model ladder up to gradient boosting, capturing … of the perfect-foresight ceiling; no model beats it overall.

A smart-charging feature on this market needs automation and UX, not machine learning. The algorithm choice moves a few euros a year; automated timing at all moves about two hundred.

Finding 2Is it the right target?

Day-ahead is a fair price for the household, and a poor proxy for real time.

Finding 1 picks hours against the day-ahead price, which is what a dynamic tariff bills. But that price is itself a forecast, so it can be checked against the price that forms at delivery: the German imbalance price (reBAP). Over … hours the systematic gap is near zero (median …), so the household is billed fairly. The variance is another matter. Real time is … as volatile as day-ahead and only … correlated with it, and the three cheapest day-ahead hours are still the cheapest at real time on just … of days.

No consumer-facing intraday product: the household gap is zero, so there is nothing to pass through. The real-time value is variance, capturable only by whoever holds the balancing position, which makes it a business-to-business aggregation play worth about … per household-equivalent. It is also earned unevenly, with … of it sitting in the worst 5% of days, so capturing it is a readiness requirement rather than a daily margin.

Finding 3Can an event move it?

The World Cup did not detectably move the German market.

Prices during match hours: … versus weather-comparable days, statistically unclear, and still unclear at … under a within-day contrast that nets out day-level seasonal drift. Demand: every subset, overnight kickoffs included, is consistent with chance in the placebo test (…).

The study states its own limits: it could only have detected a price effect of about … or larger, so the honest claim is "no large effect", not "no effect".

A method that can detect a real event is what makes this null a finding rather than a broken tool.
